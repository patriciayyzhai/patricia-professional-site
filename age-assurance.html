Age Assurance Is a Design Choice, Not Just a Compliance Check

A public-policy case for user safety, proportional regulation, and sustainable growth in the games industry.

Based on a panel at the Tencent Games Global Legal Conference · Kuala Lumpur · May 2026

At the Tencent Games Global Legal Conference in Kuala Lumpur in May 2026, I joined a panel on global age-assurance regulation, healthy gameplay, and the future of child safety in games.

Much of the regulatory debate around age assurance begins with a seemingly simple question: how can a platform know whether a user is a child? But for the games industry, the more consequential question comes next.

Once a platform knows—or has reasonable confidence—that a user is fourteen rather than twenty-four, what should change?

Age assurance is often imagined as a digital bouncer: a gate that checks whether someone is old enough to enter. That framing is too narrow. Age assurance is better understood as a design choice, grounded in user-safety principles. It enables platforms to shape communication settings, social interactions, spending mechanics, content access, and parental tools around the needs of users at different stages of development.

From age verification to age-appropriate experience

The strongest argument for age assurance is not that regulators require it. It is that many other child-safety measures depend on it. A platform cannot reliably apply age-based chat restrictions, spending safeguards, parental-consent mechanisms, or differentiated content settings without first establishing whether a user is likely to be a minor.

A mature approach should therefore ask not only whether a user is above or below a single threshold, but how different age bands may warrant different experiences. A seven-year-old, a fourteen-year-old, and a seventeen-year-old do not have identical needs, legal capacities, or patterns of play.

Safety and industry growth are not opposing objectives

Poorly designed regulation can create friction. Repeated identity checks, unnecessary collection of personal data, and inflexible age gates can undermine user trust and create barriers to access. But weak safety systems create their own costs: preventable harm, declining trust, enforcement risk, and abrupt product restrictions.

The better policy objective is not maximum friction or minimum friction. It is proportionate assurance: systems that are sufficiently effective for the risks involved, privacy-preserving in their use of data, and integrated into product design rather than added as a late compliance patch.

Safety should not be treated as the tax paid after growth. It is part of the institutional foundation that makes sustainable growth possible.

Games are not social media—but labels alone should not determine regulation

Many modern games contain features that also appear on social platforms: text and voice chat, friend systems, user-generated content, stranger matching, and persistent social spaces. These functions can create real risks, including harmful content, unwanted adult–minor contact, and exploitative interaction.

But it does not follow that games and social media should be treated as interchangeable categories. Games are generally structured around goals, rules, active participation, and shared play. These differences matter because sound regulation should respond to how a product works, not simply what it is called.

Which product functions create material risks for children, and which controls are proportionate to those risks?

Under this approach, social chat, stranger pairing, user-generated content, and monetisation mechanics may each warrant different forms of oversight. The right unit of regulation is often the risk-bearing function, not the broad product category.

Trust & Safety as the bridge from law to product

Age assurance also illustrates why Trust & Safety cannot be reduced to content moderation or incident response. Turning legal obligations into functioning products requires interpretation and coordination across privacy, legal, public policy, compliance, product, and engineering teams.

Trust & Safety sits between these domains. Its role is to ask whether a legally compliant design will actually protect users; whether a measure is workable at scale; whether an intervention is proportionate to risk; and whether the product experience remains coherent across markets.

Toward a more mature public-policy position

A credible industry position accepts that some game features create serious risks and should attract meaningful safeguards. At the same time, it insists that regulation should be precise enough to recognise differences in design, context, and user behaviour.

The future of age assurance should not be built around ever more intrusive gates. It should be built around a more thoughtful question:

Once we know that a user may be a child, how should we design the experience differently?

That is not only a compliance question. It is a product question, a public-policy question and, ultimately, a question about what responsible industry growth should look like.
